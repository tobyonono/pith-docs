## Working Together

The best projects we've been part of have one thing in common: the client felt like a collaborator, not a customer. Not because we asked them to — but because they were genuinely involved in shaping the work at the right moments.

This page explains how we run that in practice.

---

## The Pith Approach to Collaboration

We don't arrive with a fixed solution and ask for sign-off. We bring experience, strong opinions, and a clear process — and we use those alongside the client's knowledge of their audience, their brand, and their business.

Co-creation doesn't mean design by committee. It means the right people contributing at the right stage. We're transparent about where we are and what we need, and we're flexible about how we get there.

Clients are partners. The relationship works best when both sides are invested in the outcome.

## How We Communicate

<Note>
  We're async-first by design. This isn't about avoiding conversation — it's about respecting everyone's time and creating a record of decisions that doesn't disappear when a call ends. Real-time syncs are reserved for moments that actually need them.
</Note>

- **Loom** — We use Loom for video walkthroughs of designs, staging builds, and end-of-week updates. It's faster to show than to write, and clients can watch when it suits them.
- **Notion or Linear** — All project tasks, decisions, and open questions live in one shared space. Nothing important is tracked in chat or email.
- **Figma comments** — Design feedback happens directly in Figma, attached to the relevant element. This keeps feedback contextual, actionable, and easy to resolve without back-and-forth.
- **Scheduled check-ins** — Weekly or bi-weekly video calls for alignment, bigger decisions, and moments where a live conversation is genuinely more efficient than async.

## How to Give Good Feedback

Feedback is one of the highest-leverage things a client can do on a project. Good feedback moves work forward. Vague feedback creates rework.

<Tip>
  **Be specific and directional.** "The header feels too heavy" is more useful than "I don't like this." "Can we try a version with more white space?" is more useful still. Tell us what you observe and, where possible, what you'd want to try instead.
</Tip>

<Tip>
  **Reference the brief.** The best feedback connects to the goals you set at the start of the project. "This doesn't feel like it speaks to our audience" or "this is simpler than we discussed" keeps the work anchored to what matters.
</Tip>

<Tip>
  **Separate personal preference from user needs.** Both are valid — but they're different things. If something is personally not to your taste, say so. If you think it won't work for your users, say that too. It helps us make the right call.
</Tip>

## Decision-Making

Pith makes recommendations based on experience, craft, and what the work needs. We'll tell you what we think and why — including when we disagree with a direction.

Clients make final decisions. Always. We document those decisions in the project tracker so the context behind them is never lost — especially useful months later when someone asks "why did we do it this way?"

## What Makes Projects Work

In our experience, the projects that go smoothly share a few simple characteristics:

- **A clear brief** — One that defines the goal, the audience, and how success is measured. Ambiguity at the start compounds into problems later.
- **Timely feedback** — Feedback delays are the single most common cause of schedule overruns. We share this upfront, not as pressure, but as a simple truth: when we're waiting, the project is waiting.
- **Trust in the process** — The structure we follow exists because it works. Skipping steps to save time usually costs more time later.

We'll always flag early if something looks like it could affect timeline or scope. No surprises.
